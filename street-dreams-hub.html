🤝 How It Works: Four Partners, One Project

  1. 1👥 Neighbors signProperty owners Owners on each street sign a petition. Once owners of 60% of the front footage sign, the whole street shares the cost (ORC 727.06). A strong petition means Council needs only a majority vote (ORC 727.12).
  2. 2🏙️ City approvesCity Council (and the state receiver, for now) Council votes to approve the project and each property's estimated share (ORC 727.12, 727.23), plus an agreement letting the county manage it (ORC 307.15).
  3. 3💰 State may help fundOhio Public Works Commission (OPWC) The city applies for state grants. SCIP can cover up to 90% and LTIP up to 100%, and they’re competitive.
  4. 4🏛️ County buildsCounty Public Works If the county agrees, it manages the engineering, bidding and construction, working with the city.
➜ Streets fixed! Costs are spread over about 20 years on the property tax bill, starting after the work is done. See the full step-by-step roadmap

Grandview Terrace

A narrow dead-end street off Oak Hill Rd, between Standwood Ave (Oak Hill to Hazel) and Terrace Rd, with about 8–10 homes and no room to turn vehicles around. It likely needs more than resurfacing to be safe year-round, so we're looking at a separate approach for it.

🗓️ Each street decides for itself We're asking every street that needs work to make a simple go or no-go decision by our target date, the end of March 2027. Here's why: the county and state are expected to open the next round of grant applications (for 2029 funding) around July 2027. Deciding by March gives "go" streets time to gather signatures and have their paperwork ready when that window opens. Grant applications are scored, and the more impact a project has, the higher its priority, so the more streets that say "go" together, the stronger our application can be. "No-go" means that street isn't included for now, with no pressure. Each street's choice is its own and doesn't affect any other street.

🗺️ Section 1: The Roadmap to Better Streets

The 7 steps, from first meeting to finished street
Phase 1 · Get Neighbors On Board
  1. Map Your Block Pick the streets to repave, light, or upgrade. Typical items include road resurfacing, street lighting, curbs, and driveway aprons where needed (ORC 727.01). Then measure each property’s front footage. ✅ Good news: chances are neighbors already measured your front footage! Look yours up here.
  2. Establish Street Committee & Goals Form an organizing committee for your street, outline specific upgrades, and host an initial meeting.
  3. Choose Your Pathway Path A · City Fronts Costs: Needs owners of 60% front footage or 75% area (ORC 727.06). Under receivership, this path may not be available right now. Path B · County Builds (most likely path for now): Same petition. City Council approves an agreement letting the county handle this project only (ORC 307.15).
  4. Gather Signatures & File Collect signatures on official forms and submit to City Council.
Phase 2 · Review & Approvals
  1. Official Agreement & Engineering City and county sign agreements (ORC 307.15), complete engineering, and pass Resolution of Necessity.
  2. Notices & Equalization Hearing Owners receive cost estimates and have public hearing rights.
Phase 3 · Construction & Subsidies
  1. Build, Finance & Unlock Subsidies Bidding, construction, and spreading costs over multi-year tax bills while leveraging SCIP/LTIP state grants.

💡 Key Takeaway: Fixing a street is an organized journey, and neighbors stay in the driver’s seat.

🛡️ Plan for the worst case: without grant help. Grants can make a big difference, but they aren't promised. 💲 How to read the cost tiers Each tier is a possible total cost for your street's project. Your monthly and yearly numbers show what your household would pay at that cost without grant help, based on your share of the street's front footage and spread over 20 years. Use them to see what you'd be comfortable with. Grants come off the top. Neighbors' share = total cost × (100% − grant %). Example: a $250,000 project with a 50% grant leaves $125,000 for neighbors, so look at the tier closest to $125,000. Estimates don't include any financing costs; the county will confirm final figures.
📐 How your share is calculated Your share = your lot's front footage ÷ the street's total front footage, times the project cost, spread over 20 years (240 months). Example: a lot with 80 ft of frontage on a street with 2,000 ft total pays 4% (80 ÷ 2,000). At a $100,000 project cost, that's $4,000, or about $16.67 a month over 20 years. Corner lots: in these estimates, the street your house faces counts at full frontage, and the side street counts at 40% of the lot's depth. The county will confirm its own method.

🚧 Short-Term Relief for Aging Streets

While organizing for formal special assessment petitions and OPWC grants remains the long-term solution for capital paving, tactical short-term measures provide essential interim relief:

  1. Municipal Pothole Work Orders Action: Coordinate through your Ward Council Member and city service department to aggressively target deep potholes and base failures. Reality check: A temporary band-aid, but vital to prevent total road failure and vehicle damage.
  2. Drainage & Catch Basin Clearing Action: File immediate service requests to clear blocked storm drains and catch basins. Reality check: Standing water accelerates freeze-thaw cracking during winters; keeping drainage clear buys structural lifespan.

The petition & the work
Only homeowners can sign the petition, because the assessment is tied to the property. Renters are welcome to share their views with their landlord.
Yes. Every owner can sign, wherever they live. Ohio's law (ORC 727.06) only says the petition must be signed by the owners, and it doesn't mention needing a witness. We expect out-of-state owners will be able to sign by mail: we send the petition page, you sign it in ink with your name as it appears on the deed, and mail it back. Having it notarized where you live is optional but adds extra proof. We're confirming with the city exactly what they'll accept, including whether scanned or electronic signatures are OK.
Once owners of 60% of a street’s front footage sign the petition, every property along that street is included, even owners who didn’t sign (ORC 727.06). It works as a group decision for the whole street. Owners who don’t sign keep extra protection: their assessment can’t exceed one-third of their property’s value, a limit that signers give up (ORC 727.03). Every owner also gets a written estimate before any work starts and can object at a public hearing (ORC 727.13–727.17), and the cost is spread over years on the tax bill. That’s why we ask each street to decide together, with every neighbor having the numbers and facts before anyone signs.
Council votes at three points. First, it passes a resolution saying the project is needed, listing the streets, the cost split and each property’s estimated share (ORC 727.12). After owners get written notice and a chance to object at a hearing, Council votes again to go ahead (ORC 727.23). Once the work is finished and the real cost is known, Council sets the final amounts (ORC 727.25). A strong petition helps: if owners of most of the street’s frontage sign, Council needs only a majority vote instead of three-fourths. While the city is under state receivership, we’re confirming with the city and county who casts these votes for now.
